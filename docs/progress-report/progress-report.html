1. System Overview

The system is a web-based self-service ordering kiosk with integrated real-time stock inventory and transaction management for Zak's Sizzling Hub, a sizzling-plate restaurant. Customers order on an in-store touchscreen kiosk (a Raspberry Pi with a 1024 × 600 display) or on their own phones by scanning a QR code. They browse the menu by category, search or filter by price, add dishes with special instructions, choose dine in or take out, and pay at the counter (online payment through QR Ph is implemented but not yet live). Each order receives a unique order number and an estimated preparation time. On the in-store kiosk the receipt is printed on a thermal printer, and a second printer in the kitchen prints a ticket for each paid order.

Staff use a back office on a separate address. At the counter, a light point of sale takes payment for kiosk orders (cash with the change worked out, or GCash or Maya recorded with its reference number) and prints a receipt, rings up walk-in customers' orders, and cancels paid orders with a reason. Staff follow orders on a kitchen display that updates in real time and adjust stock with a recorded reason. Every dish is made from a bill of materials: when an order is paid the system deducts its raw materials (items sold as they are, such as bottled drinks, by the piece), and every open kiosk updates its sold-out items without refreshing. The Administrator additionally manages products, recipes, prices, costs and photos, imports products and recipes in bulk from CSV or Excel files, views sales analytics with CSV, Excel and PDF export, manages user accounts and roles, and prints the kiosk QR code.

A background worker checks stock levels every hour, sends low-stock e-mail alerts when e-mail is configured, and backs up the database every night to off-site cloud storage. The system is deployed on Railway as separate customer and staff web addresses, an API service, a worker and a managed PostgreSQL database. This report describes only what is present in the source code; every status in Section 4 cites the files that implement it.

5. Completed Features

The completed work covers the full ordering cycle and the back-office tools around it. The figures are low-fidelity wireframes drawn from the current code (docs/diagrams/); each names the files it is based on.

5.1 Customer ordering

The kiosk opens on a welcome screen and asks once whether the customer will dine in or take out. The menu shows one category at a time, with a category rail, keyword search and price-range chips; sold-out dishes are dimmed and update live. Customers add dishes directly or through an item sheet with quantity, quick instruction options and a note. Checkout shows the order, an estimated preparation time based on the kitchen queue, and the payment choice (Pay at counter, or QR Ph once online payment is set up). Placing the order returns a receipt with a unique order number; with QR Ph the kiosk first shows the payment QR code to scan. Inactivity triggers a warning and then resets the kiosk for the next customer.

5.2 Counter payment and kitchen

Orders awaiting payment is the counter's light point of sale, with three tabs. In the Queue, the cashier finds the order by its number and takes payment: cash, with the change worked out, or GCash or Maya, recorded with the reference number from the customer's phone. Taking payment marks the order paid, deducts the stock and creates kitchen tasks in a single database transaction; if any item has run out, nothing changes and the cashier is told before the money is kept. A receipt then shows on screen and prints on the counter computer's printer. New order rings up a walk-in customer from a menu grid, and Paid orders lists the orders in the kitchen, which the cashier can cancel with a reason. Unpaid orders can be cancelled in the Queue. Paid orders appear immediately on the kitchen display, where each dish moves from Pending to In progress to Completed, with dine-in and take-out orders distinguished. As an order appears, a ticket with its number and dishes prints on the kitchen printer. The order itself becomes Preparing when its first dish starts and Ready when the last is done; a status board on the screen at the counter then shows its number under Ready for pickup, until the kitchen presses Handed over. The payment queue updates by itself as orders are placed and paid.

5.3 Inventory

Following the panel's review, every dish is made from a bill of materials: its recipe of raw materials is required, and the kiosk shows how many servings the raw materials still allow. Items sold as they are, such as bottled drinks, are kept in a built-in Ready-made category and counted in pieces. Each raw material and ready-made item has a minimum level; the dashboard says how many are at or below it, with links to the Raw materials page already filtered, and each is queued for an e-mail alert. The Raw materials page has a search bar, a category filter, the servings of each dish, the ready-made items, and one Stock movements log of every change. The Administrator can remove a raw material after a confirmation, unless a recipe still uses it. Every manual change requires a reason and is logged with the person, the time and the before and after quantities. Barcode lookup was removed at the panel's request.

5.4 Products, analytics, users and operations

The Administrator creates and edits products with validation, photos, preparation times, recipes, ingredients, allergens and nutrition (looked up in USDA FoodData Central or calculated from the recipe), manages the menu's categories, sets each dish's special-instruction options, and imports products and their recipes in bulk from CSV or Excel after a row-by-row check. Analytics show daily, weekly and monthly sales with the average order value, top-selling products, profitability and inventory movement, each exportable as CSV, Excel or PDF, and a transaction history lists every order with its timeline. User accounts can be created, assigned a role, suspended and reactivated. The Administrator also prints a QR code that opens the kiosk on customers' phones. In operation, the worker backs up the database every night to off-site storage, and separate web addresses keep staff pages off the customer link.

5.5 Security and quality

Passwords are hashed with bcrypt, staff and administrators confirm each sign-in with a code emailed to them, staff sessions use short-lived tokens, all input is validated, responses carry security headers, only the kiosk and admin addresses may call the API, payment webhooks are verified by signature, and repeated sign-in, order and payment attempts are rate limited. The API and worker are covered by automated unit, integration and security tests (Section 8).

7. Known Issues and Limitations

7.1 Functional

  1. Ready notice on customers' phones. The status board on the counter screen tells customers their order is ready; a customer who ordered on their own phone through the QR code is not notified on the phone, whose receipt does not update by itself. Left for future development.
  2. Changing an order. When stock runs out at confirmation, the cashier is told to change the order with the customer, but no screen edits an existing order. The API provides routes to change or remove order items that the web application does not use; in practice the order is cancelled and placed again.
  3. Nutrition values are estimates. They are looked up in USDA FoodData Central (the key is set on the production API). Values calculated from a recipe use raw ingredients and do not account for cooking.
  4. Online payment not yet verified. The QR Ph flow (code on the kiosk, status check, webhook) has only been tested with PayMongo simulated; two details depend on PayMongo's live behaviour (attaching the QR Ph method with the secret key, and the location of the test-mode simulate link). The return page of the earlier hosted-checkout flow (/checkout/success) remains in the code but is no longer reached.
  5. Kitchen printing needs a browser on the printer's device. The kitchen ticket is sent by the kitchen display open (in printer mode) on the device the kitchen printer is plugged into, a Raspberry Pi or Linux computer running the print service. Other devices, such as an Android tablet, can show the kitchen display but cannot print.
  6. Account passwords. Users cannot change their own password, and temporary passwords given to new users are not required to be changed.

7.2 Security and maintenance

  1. Hosting provider incident. On 21 October 2026 Railway disclosed unauthorised access to customer environment variables and advised rotating every secret. The three token secrets have been replaced; the database password and the backup-storage and e-mail keys remain to be rotated, and Railway paused deployments during its investigation.
  2. Dependency advisories. npm audit reports 18 advisories in production dependencies (3 critical, 9 high, 6 moderate), all with fixes available. The critical advisory in Next.js concerns next/og, which the system does not use; the others should still be resolved by upgrading.
  3. Browser token storage. Staff access tokens are kept in browser storage, which a cross-site scripting flaw could expose; the impact is limited by the 15-minute token lifetime and the httpOnly refresh cookie.
  4. No automated tests for the web interface. The screens were checked manually in a browser.
